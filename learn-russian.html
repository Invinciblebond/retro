The Russian Alphabet — Азбука

There are 33 letters in the Russian alphabet: 10 vowels, 21 consonants and 2 signs (ь, ъ). Russian is an East Slavic language, a close relative of Ukrainian and Belarusian, spoken by roughly 250 million people. Its alphabet is Cyrillic, and most of its letters stand for a sound you already make in English.

Tap a letter to hear its name and the sample word, then say it back out loud, copying the speaker as closely as you can.

Three rules that make it click

The letters above get you reading. These explain why words don't always sound letter-for-letter.

Stress changes vowels

Only one syllable is stressed. An unstressed о relaxes to "ah": молоко́ is mah-lah-KOH, not moh-loh-KOH.

Final consonants lose their voice

At the end of a word б д г з ж в come out as п т к с ш ф: хлеб (bread) sounds like KHLYEP.

Some vowels soften

е ё и ю я (and ь) make the consonant before them "soft", with the tongue lifted toward a y-sound: нет is NYET.

Ё often loses its dots

Outside textbooks, ё is usually printed as е: you'll see ёж written as еж. Context tells you it's still "yo".

More about the Russian alphabet

In the 860s two Byzantine brothers, the monks Cyril and Methodius, invented the first Slavic writing system so they could translate the Bible for Slavic peoples. They called it Glagolitic, from the Old Slavic glagolŭ, "word" or "sound". Within a few decades their students replaced it with a simpler script built largely on Greek letters, and named it Cyrillic in Cyril's honour.

Cyrillic reached Kievan Rus' with Christianity in 988. For centuries the written language was Church Slavonic; Peter the Great introduced a simplified "civil script" in 1708 for science and everyday books, using the Moscow dialect as its base. The 1918 spelling reform then dropped four letters (і, ѣ, ѳ, ѵ), leaving the 33-letter alphabet that is still used today.

Core Words — Основны́е слова́

The words that turn up in almost every conversation: greetings, people, time, numbers, food and the verbs you need to say what you want. Every word and example comes with an English pronunciation guide. Tap a word to hear it, or tap the example to hear the whole sentence.

Reading the word cards

Gender: masc, fem, neut

Every noun has a gender, and you can usually hear it: a consonant ending is masculine (дом), -а/-я is feminine (вода́), -о/-е is neuter (молоко́). Words ending in ь can go either way.

No "am", "is", "are"

Russian drops "to be" in the present tense. Я студе́нт is literally "I student", and that's perfectly correct.

Ты vs вы

ты is "you" for friends, family and children. Use вы with strangers, elders, anyone at work, and for more than one person.
