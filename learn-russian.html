The Russian Alphabet — Азбука

There are 33 letters in the Russian alphabet: 10 vowels, 21 consonants and 2 signs (ь, ъ). Russian is an East Slavic language, a close relative of Ukrainian and Belarusian, spoken by roughly 250 million people. Its alphabet is Cyrillic, and most of its letters stand for a sound you already make in English.

Three rules that make it click

The letters above get you reading. These explain why words don't always sound letter-for-letter.

Stress changes vowels

Only one syllable is stressed. An unstressed о relaxes to "ah": молоко́ is mah-lah-KOH, not moh-loh-KOH.

Final consonants lose their voice

At the end of a word б д г з ж в come out as п т к с ш ф: хлеб (bread) sounds like KHLYEP.

Some vowels soften

е ё и ю я (and ь) make the consonant before them "soft", with the tongue lifted toward a y-sound: нет is NYET.

Ё often loses its dots

Outside textbooks, ё is usually printed as е: you'll see ёж written as еж. Context tells you it's still "yo".

More about the Russian alphabet

In the 860s two Byzantine brothers, the monks Cyril and Methodius, invented the first Slavic writing system so they could translate the Bible for Slavic peoples. They called it Glagolitic, from the Old Slavic glagolŭ, "word" or "sound". Within a few decades their students replaced it with a simpler script built largely on Greek letters, and named it Cyrillic in Cyril's honour.

Cyrillic reached Kievan Rus' with Christianity in 988. For centuries the written language was Church Slavonic; Peter the Great introduced a simplified "civil script" in 1708 for science and everyday books, using the Moscow dialect as its base. The 1918 spelling reform then dropped four letters (і, ѣ, ѳ, ѵ), leaving the 33-letter alphabet that is still used today.

Core Words — Основны́е слова́

The words that turn up in almost every conversation: greetings, people, time, numbers, food and the verbs you need to say what you want. Every word and example comes with an English pronunciation guide. Tap a word to hear it, or tap the example to hear the whole sentence.

Reading the word cards

Gender: masc, fem, neut

Every noun has a gender, and you can usually hear it: a consonant ending is masculine (дом), -а/-я is feminine (вода́), -о/-е is neuter (молоко́). Words ending in ь can go either way.

No "am", "is", "are"

Russian drops "to be" in the present tense. Я студе́нт is literally "I student", and that's perfectly correct.

Ты vs вы

ты is "you" for friends, family and children. Use вы with strangers, elders, anyone at work, and for more than one person.

Russian grammar has a reputation for being hard. It's fairer to say it's different: English uses word order and little helper words (the, of, to, will, have) to show who does what, while Russian changes the endings of the words themselves. Unit 1

How a Russian sentence works

Five things English speakers notice in their very first Russian sentences: the missing "the", the missing "is", the loose word order, questions that look like statements, and double negatives.

01There are no articlesНет арти́клей No "a", "an" or "the". One word covers all three, and context does the rest.

English makes you choose between a book, the book and just book every time you use a noun. Russian simply has кни́га. That is not a shortcut or slang: articles do not exist in the language at all, so a sentence like Кни́га на столе́ is complete and correct.

When you translate into English you add the article yourself, based on what makes sense. When you speak Russian, the hardest part is resisting the urge to insert something where "the" would go.

So how do Russians show "a" versus "the"?

  • Word order. Information the listener already knows tends to come first; new information comes last. Кни́га на столе́ ("The book is on the table": we both know which book) versus На столе́ кни́га ("On the table there's a book": the book is news).
  • This / that. э́тот (this) and тот (that) point at a specific thing, much like a strong "the".
  • "One" / "some". оди́н (one) or како́й-то (some… or other) can introduce "a certain" person or thing in a story.
Try it: translate into Russian
  1. a cat / the cat / cats are nice animals (just the noun) → кот / кот / ко́шки
  2. "The water is cold." (вода́ = water, холо́дная = cold) → Вода́ холо́дная.
  3. "There's a house over there." (дом, там) → Там дом.
02"To be" disappears in the presentСвязка «быть» "I am a student" is just "I student". The verb returns in the past and future.

The verb быть means to be, but in the present tense Russians leave it out. Where English needs am, is, are, Russian just puts the two ideas side by side: Я студе́нт, literally "I student". This sounds like broken English to us, but in Russian it is perfectly formal, correct and complete.

In writing, when both halves are nouns, a dash often stands where "is" would be: Москва́ — столи́ца Росси́и ("Moscow — capital of Russia"). You don't pronounce the dash; just leave a tiny pause.

03Word order is flexibleПоря́док слов Endings show who does what, so words can move. Position shows emphasis instead.

In English, order carries the grammar. "The dog bites the man" and "The man bites the dog" are different news stories, and the only clue is which noun comes first. Russian marks the doer and the receiver with endings (the case system, Unit 3), so the words can be rearranged without changing who did what.

Take "Mom loves Dad". ма́ма is in its dictionary form (the doer), and па́па becomes па́пу (the one being loved). Both orders mean the same thing:

What the order does change: emphasis

The default order is subject – verb – object, just like English, and it's always safe. When you move things around, you are highlighting something: the newest or most important information goes at the end. English does this with stress and extra words ("It was Ivan who read it"); Russian just moves Ivan to the end.

  • Adjectives normally come before the noun: но́вый дом (a new house).
  • Short pronoun objects often sit before the verb: Я тебя́ люблю́ ("I you love") is the most natural way to say "I love you".
  • Questions don't flip the order the way "Are you…?" does in English (rule 04).
Rule of thumbWhile you're learning, use English order and put the thing you want to stress last. You'll sound natural far more often than not.
04Asking questionsВопро́сы A yes/no question is a statement with a rising voice. Question words go at the front.

English builds questions by rearranging words and adding do: "You live here" → "Do you live here?". Russian keeps the sentence exactly as it is and changes only the melody. The voice jumps up sharply on the word you're asking about, then drops right after it. In writing, only the question mark shows the difference.

Moving the rise changes the question, just as stress does in English: rise on ты = "Do you live here?", rise on живёшь = "Do you live here (or just visit)?"

The particle ли

More formal or bookish questions add ли right after the word being questioned, and it doubles as English "whether / if" in reported questions. You'll meet it constantly in literature — Raskolnikov's famous Тварь ли я дрожа́щая… ("Am I a trembling creature…?") in Lesson 4 is built on it.

05Negation and double negativesОтрица́ние не goes right before what you deny. "Nobody", "never", "nothing" still need не.

To make something negative, put не ("not") directly in front of the word you are denying. Usually that's the verb, and there is no "do" or "does" to add: Я не зна́ю — "I not know" = "I don't know". не is unstressed and glued to the next word in speech: nee-ZNAH-yoo.

Because it negates only its neighbour, moving не changes the meaning:

нет — "no" and "there isn't"

нет answers a question ("No"). It also means "there is no / there isn't", and then the missing thing goes into the genitive case (rule 16): Здесь нет воды́ — "There's no water here."

Double negatives are required

In standard English "I don't know nothing" is a mistake. In Russian it's the only correct way. Every "no-" word (nobody, nothing, never, nowhere) starts with ни-, and the verb must still have не:

Unit 2

Nouns, pronouns and adjectives

Before cases, learn the "dictionary forms": every noun has a gender, every adjective has to match it, and "my", "your", "his" behave differently from English.

06Noun genderРод существи́тельных Every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter, and the last letter almost always tells you which.

In English only people and animals have a gender (he for a man, she for a woman, it for a table). In Russian every noun is grammatically masculine, feminine or neuter, including tables, ideas and Tuesdays. It has nothing to do with meaning: a table (стол) is masculine, a lamp (ла́мпа) is feminine and a window (окно́) is neuter. The good news is that you can nearly always tell by looking at the last letter.

Why it matters

Gender decides the form of every adjective, possessive and past-tense verb that goes with the noun, and which pronoun replaces it. There is no neutral "it" for objects: a table is "he" and a car is "she".

The exceptions worth knowing

  • Men ending in -а / -я are masculine, because meaning wins: па́па (dad), дя́дя (uncle), мужчи́на (man), and nicknames like Ми́ша, Ва́ня, Ро́дя. They still change their endings like feminine nouns.
  • A handful of neuter nouns end in -мя: и́мя (name), вре́мя (time).
  • Borrowed words ending in a vowel usually never change and are neuter: кафе́, метро́, такси́, радио. The famous exception is ко́фе, which is traditionally masculine.

Ты or вы?

English once had this too: thou was the familiar "you" and you the polite one. Russian kept both.

  • ты — family, friends, children, pets, God in prayer, and people your own age in casual settings.
  • вы — strangers, older people, anyone at work, shop staff, officials, and any group, even of close friends.

Getting it wrong in the ты direction can sound rude or overfamiliar. If in doubt, use вы and wait: the older or more senior person usually suggests switching with Дава́й на ты ("Let's say ты").

Polite вы always takes plural verb forms, even for one person — exactly like English "you are", never "you is": Вы бы́ли пра́вы ("You were right"). In letters, it's often capitalised as a courtesy: Вы.

Names go with itFormal вы pairs with first name + patronymic (the father's name + -ович / -овна): Родио́н Рома́нович = "Rodion, son of Roman". Informal ты pairs with a nickname: Ро́дя. You'll hear both for the same man in Lesson 4.

Unit 3

The case system

The heart of Russian grammar. Nouns, adjectives and pronouns change their endings to show their job in the sentence. It looks like a lot of tables, but there are only six jobs, and English already does this with a handful of words.

13What a case is (and the six of them)Паде́жи English does this with he / him / his. Russian does it with every noun, six ways.

You already use cases. In English you say "He sees me", but "I see him", and "That's his car". It's the same person, but the word changes depending on its job: doing the action, receiving it, or owning something. English only does this with pronouns (I/me/my, she/her, they/them/their, who/whom/whose). Russian does it with every noun, adjective and pronoun, and it has six jobs instead of three.

That's why Russian needs fewer little words like "of", "to", "with", and why word order can be loose (rule 03): the ending already says who is doing what.

14Nominative: the subjectИмени́тельный паде́ж The dictionary form. Used for whoever or whatever does the action, and after "this is".

The nominative is the "default" form you find in a dictionary and have been using in all the rules so far. It answers кто? (who?) or что? (what?) and is used for:

  • The subject — whoever is doing the action or being described: Студе́нт чита́ет.
  • Both halves of an "X is Y" sentence in the present: Он — врач.
  • After э́то and in naming or labelling: Э́то Москва́.
  • The thing you have in у меня́ есть… and the thing you like in мне нра́вится… (rules 30 and 32), because in Russian those are the grammatical subjects.

Which family is a verb in?

  • Most verbs in -ить are 2nd conjugation (говори́ть, люби́ть, звони́ть).
  • Most verbs in -ать / -ять are 1st (чита́ть, де́лать, гуля́ть), with a few famous exceptions: слы́шать (hear), держа́ть (hold).
  • Verbs in -еть go both ways: уме́ть → уме́ешь (1st) but смотре́ть → смо́тришь (2nd).
  • The safest habit: learn every new verb with its я and ты forms, the way English learners learn "go, went, gone".

The я form can change its consonant (2nd conjugation)

In the я form only, some consonants shift: б п в м ф add an л (люби́ть → люблю́), д / з → ж (ви́деть → ви́жу), с → ш (проси́ть → прошу́), т → ч (плати́ть → плачу́), ст → щ (прости́ть → прощу́). The other five forms are regular: лю́бишь, ви́дишь.

For things that may really happen, use е́сли with the future in both halves (English uses the present after "if"): Е́сли за́втра бу́дет дождь, мы оста́немся до́ма.

Unreal conditions: бы + past tense

For imagined or impossible situations, put the particle бы in both halves and use the past tense form. There's no difference between present and past: the same sentence means "If I had money, I would buy a house" and "If I'd had money, I would have bought a house". Context decides.

  • Polite requests: Я бы хоте́л… (I'd like…) is softer than Я хочу́.
  • Advice: Я бы на твоём ме́сте… (If I were you…).

что́бы: purpose and wishes about others

  • In order to + infinitive: Я пришёл, что́бы помо́чь (I came to help).
  • Wanting someone else to do something: English says "I want you to come". Russian says "I want, so that you came": Я хочу́, что́бы ты пришёл. The verb is always in the past form.

Reading & Pronunciation — Преступле́ние и наказа́ние

Time to read real Russian: Fyodor Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment, published in 1866. A penniless former student in St Petersburg, Rodion Raskolnikov, convinces himself that an extraordinary person has the right to cross moral lines for a greater good, and then has to live with what he does. It is one of the most famous novels ever written, and every line below is Dostoevsky's own original Russian.

This is hard, literary Russian, so nothing is left to chance. Each passage is split into short lines, and every line has stress marks, an English pronunciation guide and its own translation. Start with the sound rules just below, then open an excerpt, listen to it, and read it aloud line by line until it flows.

The sound rules behind the spelling

Russian is written almost phonetically, but not quite. These twelve patterns explain nearly every place where a word doesn't sound the way it's spelled. Tap any word to hear it.

01One stressed syllable, and it moves

Each word has one strong syllable, and nothing in normal spelling shows where it is. Stress can also shift between forms of the same word, and it can change the meaning entirely.

02Unstressed о sounds like "ah"

Only a stressed о is a real "oh". Right before the stress it relaxes to "ah"; further away it's a quick "uh". This is called akanye, and it's the single biggest giveaway of a foreign accent.

03Unstressed е and я sound like "ee"

Away from the stress, е and я shrink to a short "ee" (ikanye). я at the very end of a word keeps a hint of "yah".

04Final consonants go voiceless

At the end of a word, б в г д ж з are said as п ф к т ш с. English does a softer version of this; Russian does it every time.

05Consonant clusters match each other

In a cluster, the first consonant copies the voicing of the second: вч → "fch", сд → "zd", жк → "shk". Short prepositions like в, с, к glue onto the next word and follow the same rule.

06г is "v" in -ого / -его

In the endings -ого and -его (and in его́, сего́дня), the г is pronounced "v". Very common in the excerpts.

07ч becomes "sh" in a few words

что and чтобы start with "sht". In коне́чно and ску́чно the чн is "shn".

08Silent letters

Some clusters are too awkward to say in full, so one consonant drops, like the "t" in English "listen". The в in чу́вство, the д in се́рдце and the л in со́лнце are all silent.

09-тся and -ться are "tsa"

The reflexive ending on verbs is written two ways but always said as one quick "tsa".

10Hard and soft consonants

Almost every consonant comes in a pair. The soft one is said with the middle of your tongue raised toward the roof of your mouth, as if about to say "y". A soft sign ь or a following е ё и ю я makes it soft, and it can change the word.

11ы is not и

и is "ee". ы is made further back: say "ee" while pulling your tongue back as for "oo", without rounding your lips. Swapping them changes the word.

12Melody: statements fall, questions leap

A statement falls at the end. A yes/no question uses the same words but jumps up sharply on the key word. Exclamations start high and stay high. Read the dashes — in dialogue as short pauses, and ... as a trailing, hesitant voice.

How to practise with these excerpts

1 · Listen first

Play the whole passage at slow speed with the text in front of you. Don't try to understand; just follow the sounds.

2 · Line by line

Tap each line, then read it aloud straight away, copying rhythm and stress. Use the pronunciation guide only when stuck.

3 · Hide the helpers

Switch off the pronunciation, then the English. When you can read the Russian with the right stresses and roughly know what it means, move on.

4 · Shadow it

Play at normal speed and speak along with the voice, a split second behind. It's the fastest way to fix rhythm and melody.

What the novel looked like in 1866

Dostoevsky wrote before the 1918 spelling reform (see Lesson 1). His first readers saw extra letters: ъ after every final consonant, ѣ ("yat", said like е) and і (said like и). Here is the first sentence roughly as it was printed, with the changed letters highlighted:

Въ началѣ іюля, въ чрезвычайно жаркое время, подъ вечеръ, одинъ молодой человѣкъ вышелъ изъ своей каморки, которую нанималъ отъ жильцовъ въ С—мъ переулкѣ, на улицу и медленно, какъ бы въ нерѣшимости, отправился къ К—ну мосту.

Nothing about the sound changed: ъ at the end of a word was already silent, ѣ and е had merged centuries earlier, and і was just another way of writing и. The reform removed letters people had to memorise but no longer heard. The printed Russian texts are also public domain: Dostoevsky died in 1881.
